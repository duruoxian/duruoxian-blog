Text 1

When the results of the national mathematics test were published last month, two facts stood out. Average scores, after a decade of decline, had stabilized. And the gap between the highest- and lowest-performing schools, after a decade of narrowing, had widened again. Both facts, oddly, were claimed as victories — by the government, for the first, and by the teaching unions, for the second's implicit indictment of funding cuts.

Behind the dueling press releases lies an old and unresolved argument: what, exactly, are standardized tests for? For their defenders, tests are the public's window into a system it pays for and cannot otherwise see. Remove them, and the weakest schools lose the one pressure that reformers can apply from outside; the classroom, as one former inspector put it, "goes dark." For critics, the tests measure a narrow band of ability, reward teaching to the test, and convert every result into a ranking that tells parents more about a school's catchment than its classrooms.

Both sides have evidence. Studies that follow identical students across exam years find real gains in tested subjects — and real declines in subjects that are not tested, from music to civics. Defenders reply that any measuring tool distorts what it measures, and that the alternative — inspection, coursework, trust — distorts more while showing less.

There is, however, a third position, and it is quietly gaining ground: keep the tests, but change what is done with them. Under this view, the sin is not measurement but the high-stakes conversion of results into league tables, staff bonuses and closure threats. Several countries have moved in this direction — publishing school results as bands rather than exact scores, sampling students so that no single school is named, or testing a random subset each year. Officials get their trend lines; teachers get their classrooms back.

The experiments are too young to judge. What is already clear is that the argument is not really about arithmetic. It is about who a school answers to — the parent choosing where to live, the inspector with a checklist, or the pupils who, unlike everyone else in the debate, do not get a say.

Text 2

Ask what sleep is for, and most people answer in the language of repair: the body rests, restores, and rises renewed. The answer is not wrong, but it is incomplete, and the missing half has become one of the liveliest areas in neuroscience. During deep sleep, the brain, far from idling, replays the day. In rats taught a new maze, the same neurons that fired while learning fired again in the same order, compressed and accelerated, hours later — as though the animal were rehearsing in its sleep. Humans appear to do the same; brain imaging shows activity during sleep that mirrors patterns from the day's learning.

The practical consequences are immediate. Study after study finds that material learned before a night of sleep survives better than material learned in the morning before a full day of interference. Sleep, in other words, is not merely the interval between study sessions; it is part of the studying. This may explain a puzzling finding: students who sacrifice sleep for extra revision typically do worse, not better, on the exam they were revising for.

Not all sleep is equal, however. The dense, slow waves of deep sleep appear suited to consolidating facts and skills, while the vivid, dreaming phase — REM sleep — has been linked to a subtler operation: extracting rules. Subjects exposed to hidden patterns perform markedly better at detecting those patterns after a night rich in REM than after an equivalent period awake, as though the dreaming brain were testing the material against older memories and drawing out the general principle.

This emerging picture carries a warning for the modern bedroom. Alcohol suppresses REM; screens postpone the onset of deep sleep; irregular schedules scramble both. None of this adds a new product to sell, but it quietly reframes an old idea. Sleep is not the absence of learning. It is where some of the learning happens — and unlike most of what happens in the brain, it is available to everyone, free, tonight.

Text 3

The appeal of gig work was always framed as freedom: log in when you like, log off when you like, be your own boss. And for some — students topping up income, parents fitting hours around school runs — it is exactly that. But as platform work has scaled, researchers have documented a different reality taking shape behind the app: the algorithmic manager, which assigns tasks, sets prices, rates workers and, when its numbers dip too low, quietly removes them. No manager has ever controlled a workforce with such granularity and such indifference.

The classic defense is that gig workers are independent contractors who can decline any task. The reply, increasingly accepted by courts, is that declining is often not realistic. Acceptance rates feed into the algorithm; fall below a threshold, and premium tasks — or any tasks — dry up. Independence that cannot be exercised without penalty is, one judge wrote recently, "a legal fiction."

Reform proposals divide into two camps. The first reclassifies gig workers as employees, granting minimum wage, sick pay and collective bargaining. Platforms warn this would destroy the flexibility that makes the model work, and point to markets where reclassification was followed by reduced service and higher prices. The second camp keeps the contractor label but adds a floor: portable benefits that follow the worker across platforms, minimum earnings standards, and — most radically — a right to human review of algorithmic decisions, so that a deactivated worker can appeal to a person rather than a formula.

The second approach is cheaper and slower, and its critics call it half a loaf. Yet it addresses what is genuinely new about the situation. Workers have always faced arbitrary bosses; what is new is a boss that cannot be spoken to at all. A right to explanation — to know why the algorithm said no — costs platforms little and restores something the app removed: the possibility of being heard. Employment law took a century to protect workers from bad managers. The task now is to extend that protection to managers made of code.

Text 4

Few environmental messages have lodged as deeply as the recycling arrow. Sort your plastics, the public has been told for decades, and they will come back as new bottles. The reality is narrower. Of the seven resin codes stamped on plastic products, only two — the polyethylene terephthalate of drink bottles and the high-density polyethylene of milk jugs — are recycled at scale in most countries. The rest are typically downgraded into lumber and carpet, shipped abroad, or landfilled. A recent audit found that less than a tenth of the plastic ever produced has been recycled, against more than three-quarters of the aluminum.

Why plastic resists is chemistry, not apathy. Aluminum melts and re-forms without losing quality, essentially forever. Plastics are chains of long molecules, and every melt shortens the chains: a food-grade bottle becomes a fence post, a fence post becomes filler, and filler has nowhere left to go. Recycling plastic, unlike recycling metal, is less a loop than a staircase — each descent a little lower in quality and value.

This arithmetic has begun to reshape policy. Dozens of jurisdictions have restricted single-use plastics outright, on the logic that the cleanest ton of waste is the ton never produced. Deposit-return schemes, which pay consumers for returning bottles, deliver streams clean enough for genuine bottle-to-bottle recycling, and have achieved return rates above ninety percent where fully implemented. Chemical recycling — breaking polymers back into their molecular building blocks — remains expensive and energy-hungry, though pilot plants are multiplying.

None of this makes household sorting pointless; contaminated streams sink even the best systems. But it does correct the story we tell ourselves. The arrow on the package is not a promise of endless rebirth. It is an instruction that works only when everything downstream — the chemistry, the markets, the policy — cooperates. The durable lesson of fifty years of recycling is that materials are not interchangeable, and that the cheapest moment to manage waste is before it exists.

Why We Procrastinate

Procrastination is usually described as a time-management problem, and the standard advice follows: buy a calendar, break tasks into steps, reward yourself. Yet the evidence points elsewhere. When researchers measure chronic procrastinators, they do not find people who cannot tell time; they find people managing uncomfortable feelings. (41) ____

The task that gets postponed is rarely the longest one. It is the one that carries the strongest threat — of failure, of judgment, of revealing that we are not as capable as we hoped. (42) ____

A second finding is that procrastination is short-term mood repair. Putting off the report delivers an instant hit of relief, and the brain, which heavily discounts future costs, takes the deal. (43) ____

This explains why the standard advice so often fails. A calendar does nothing about the fear; if anything, an unfinished plan makes the task loom larger. (44) ____

What does work is shrinking the emotional stakes. (45) ____ And self-forgiveness — paradoxically — helps: students who forgave themselves for delaying one exam procrastinated less on the next.

A. The longer the delay, the heavier the task feels; the heavier it feels, the stronger the urge to delay it again. B. In other words, we are not avoiding work; we are avoiding anxiety, and the work merely comes attached to it. C. Telling yourself you will write a "perfect" first draft guarantees delay; deciding to write a bad one clears the way to a good one. D. Procrastinators are also more likely to be perfectionists, which makes every task feel larger than life. E. That is why warnings about distant deadlines so rarely change behavior: tomorrow's regret is no match for today's relief. F. The same dynamics explain why we tidy our desks at exam time — any avoidance can feel productive. G. Once started, however, most people report that the task is less unpleasant than expected.

小作文参考范文

Dear Zhang Wei,

I was sorry to hear that the pressure of preparation is getting to you — but please don't give up now. Anxiety at this stage is almost universal; it is a sign you care, not a sign you are failing.

Two things may help. First, cut the mountain into steps: replace "finish the syllabus" with three concrete tasks a day, and tick them off. Progress you can see calms the mind. Second, protect your sleep and take one evening off each week — a rested brain remembers more than an exhausted one.

You have prepared longer and better than you realize. Trust the process, and write to me whenever it gets heavy.

Yours, Li Ming

大作文参考范文

The picture shows two climbers facing the same mountain. One gazes up at the distant peak and sighs that it is impossibly high; the other, rope already fastened, has begun the ascent, his first step marked on the rock below. The caption reads: "The road lies beneath your feet."

The contrast captures a habit of mind that quietly ruins more plans than any lack of talent: measuring a goal by its total height instead of by its next step. To the first climber, the summit is one overwhelming mass; to the second, it is a series of ordinary moves. Nothing about the mountain has changed — only the way it is seen. Most grand ambitions fail not at the summit but at the base, where they are abandoned as "impossible" before a single step is taken.

The lesson applies far beyond mountains. A thesis is written page by page; vocabulary is accumulated word by word. The second climber's advantage is not courage but method: he has made the task small enough to begin. Peaks, after all, are climbed the way everything worthwhile is done — one foothold at a time.
