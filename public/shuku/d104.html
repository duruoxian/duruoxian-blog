Text 1

For the companies that dominate the modern web, attention is the raw material of profit. The average user now checks a phone dozens of times a day, and each glance is sold, in an auction that lasts milliseconds, to an advertiser. Economists call this the attention economy, and its central transaction is simple: platforms assemble audiences, advertisers bid for them, and users, who pay nothing in money, pay in something scarcer. "If you are not paying for the product," the industry saying goes, "you are the product."

Defenders of the model point out that advertising has funded free media for two centuries, and that panic about new media is old: novels, radio and television were each accused of rotting young minds, and civilization survived. There is something to this — the evidence that screens damage well-being is weaker and more mixed than the loudest warnings suggest.

But the comparison flatters the present. A paperback cannot check whether the reader has put it down, and renegotiate. Apps are engineered, with the help of behavioral science and constant experiments, to resist being put down: infinite feeds, autoplay, notifications timed to the hours when willpower is lowest. Against such designs, advice about "digital discipline," whatever its merits, treats a structural problem as a matter of private virtue — like asking swimmers to out-kick a current.

This is why the most serious proposals now focus on the design of products rather than the weakness of users. Requiring platforms to offer versions without engagement-maximizing defaults — feeds sorted by time rather than by predicted interest, autoplay off unless requested — would forbid nothing. It would simply return to the user a choice that has quietly been made for them. Early trials of such "attention-preserving" settings report modest declines in use and, notably, little fall in user satisfaction, which suggests that much of the extra screen time was consumption no one actually wanted.

Critics reply that regulation always lags technology and that firms will design around any rule. Perhaps. But car safety did not begin with drivers being urged to concentrate harder; it began with requiring seatbelts. The attention economy is unlikely to be abolished, nor need it be. The reasonable goal is narrower: that the product — whichever of us it is — should at least be consulted.

Text 2

When more than sixty British companies announced in 2022 that they would trial a four-day working week, skeptics predicted chaos: backlogs, furious clients, and pay cuts by the back door. Six months later, most of the participating firms had kept the schedule, and the majority reported that revenue had held steady or improved, while staff turnover — the rate at which employees quit — fell by more than half. The organizers argued that the results confirmed what smaller studies had long hinted: that a fifth of the working week, in many offices, is spent on meetings, email and other rituals that produce little of value, and that cutting hours forces firms to cut the waste as well.

Such numbers are striking, but they should be read with care. The firms that sign up for such trials are, almost by definition, the ones whose managers believe the idea can work; companies that expected to suffer did not volunteer. Economists call this selection bias, and it means the average participating firm is a flattering advertisement for the policy. The trials also concentrated on white-collar work, where output is measured in projects rather than hours. A hospital ward, a restaurant or a call center cannot simply close on Friday and reopen on Monday: coverage must be maintained, which means either hiring more staff — expensive — or spreading the same workers more thinly — exhausting.

None of this settles the argument against a shorter week; it only defines its limits. Where work is project-based and autonomous, the four-day week looks less like a gift to staff and more like a productivity technique, comparable to any other attempt to cut low-value meetings and interrupt-driven work. Where staffing coverage is the essence of the job, the same policy becomes a scheduling problem with real costs attached — and the honest answer is that it will be solved, if at all, slowly and unevenly.

The deeper lesson of the trials may be less about days than about discretion. Firms reported that the biggest gains came not from the extra day itself but from the redesign it forced: fewer meetings, shorter ones, fewer interruptions. That redesign was available all along. It took a change in working hours to make firms take it.

Text 3

Few tools have entered the laboratory as quickly as machine learning. Systems trained on known examples can now propose new molecules, classify galaxies and, most famously, predict the folded shapes of proteins — a problem that had resisted decades of human effort. Judged by speed alone, the case is closed: what once took a graduate student a year of bench work can take a model an afternoon.

Speed, however, is not the same as understanding, and this is where the enthusiasm meets its first serious qualification. A trained model typically reports an answer without reasons; its millions of internal parameters do not translate into an explanation a scientist can check. In fields where the mechanism matters — toxicology, for instance, where regulators must know why a compound harms — a correct prediction with no account of causes may speed the work while leaving its substance untouched. The model, in such settings, is less an oracle than a very fast filing clerk that happens to make good guesses.

There is also the quieter problem of validation. A striking recent analysis found that a notable share of machine-learning papers in materials science reported "discoveries" of crystals that, on re-examination, were already in the database or were physically impossible. The failures were not caused by malice but by workflow: models are easy to run and hard to check, and excitement outruns verification. Science has an old remedy — replication — but replication is slow, unglamorous and poorly rewarded, which is precisely why the bottleneck now lies there.

None of this argues against the technology; it argues against a certain story about it. The plausible future is not machines that replace scientists but machines that reshape their working day: generating candidate experiments, flagging anomalies, automating the parts of research that are closer to bookkeeping than to thought. The scarce resources will remain what they have always been — good questions, careful measurement, and the patience to find out when the fast answer is wrong. In that sense, the newest tool in the laboratory changes less than the headlines imply: it moves the bottleneck; it does not remove the need for judgment.

Text 4

On a hot afternoon the difference between a shaded street and an exposed one can exceed ten degrees Celsius, which is why cities from Paris to Sydney have committed to ambitious tree-planting targets. Canopy — the layer of leaves over a street — cools by shade and by transpiration, filters the air and, several studies suggest, lifts both nearby mood and property values. Few urban investments promise so much for so little, at least on paper.

The paper, however, flatters some neighborhoods and ignores others. Surveys of major cities repeatedly find the same pattern: the wealthier the district, the denser its canopy. Older suburbs grew up with trees; denser districts, often poorer, were built around roads and parking, and their residents now suffer the heat that the canopy would have softened. Worse, new planting can accelerate the very disparity it is meant to fix. Rents near a well-tended park rise, long-time residents are priced out, and the beneficiaries of the new shade are those who can already afford it — a dynamic researchers have named green gentrification.

The deeper difficulty is not planting but keeping. A sapling requires watering through its first summers, protection and pruning; a mature canopy requires budgets measured in decades. Yet municipal programs are typically judged — and funded — by trees planted, a number that is easy to announce and cheap to reach, while the quiet work of keeping trees alive attracts neither headlines nor budgets. In some cities a large fraction of new plantings die within a few years, and the counter is quietly reset.

This suggests a modest shift in how such programs are scored. Surviving canopy in ten years, not saplings in spring, is the meaningful figure; maintenance, not launch ceremonies, is where money changes outcomes. Some cities now pay community groups a small annual sum per tree kept alive, an arrangement that turns residents from spectators into wardens. It is less photogenic than officials and shovels at a groundbreaking ceremony. It is also the version that, ten years on, still casts shade.

大作文参考范文

The picture presents a family dinner that looks familiar to many of us: dishes remain untouched, and every member — old and young — bends over a glowing screen. The caption reads: "The farthest distance is between you and me, sitting face to face."

The cartoon mirrors a quiet change in family life. Smartphones were meant to bring people closer, yet paradoxically they often install an invisible wall at the very table where intimacy used to grow. When each person retreats into a private feed, the shared meal — a daily ritual through which families exchange feelings — is reduced to several silent individuals eating side by side. What is lost is not merely conversation, but the sense of being attended to.

Technology itself is not the villain; how we use it is. Simple rules — phones away at dinner, a walk after the meal — cost little yet reclaim the ground screens have occupied. The farthest distance is not measured in kilometers but in attention. Looking up from our screens, we may find that the people worth browsing are right in front of us.
